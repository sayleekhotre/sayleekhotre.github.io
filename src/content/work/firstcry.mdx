<StorySection num="01" title="Project *overview*">
  <p class="lead">FirstCry, an e-commerce platform for kids’ clothing, essentials, and accessories. It’s designed for users who often shop with curiosity to buy and in search for the right size, age-fit, or occasion-specific products.</p>

  <ul class="points">
    <li>Help parents quickly discover, compare, and buy the right products without feeling overwhelmed.</li>
    <li>Shopping for kids is often time-sensitive and emotionally driven.</li>
    <li>Improving how users discover and decide on products, by restructuring categories.</li>
    <li>Creating interactive moments like “View Similar” or GPT-driven suggestions.</li>
  </ul>
</StorySection>

<StorySection num="02" title="Understanding design *problem*">
  <p>The users know they need to buy full attire, but have no clarity.</p>
  <p><em>Will customized size, color experience make user buy the product?</em></p>
  <p>For appearance-conscious users, often led to returns with the reason <strong>“didn’t look good on me.”</strong> So here the design opportunity was to shape:</p>

  <ul class="points">
    <li>The platform lacks clear categorization and decision cues, making discovery confusing.</li>
    <li>Repeated searching and unclear filters.</li>
    <li>Product categories like essentials, beauty, or add-ons don’t align well with age-based filters, causing misdirection.</li>
  </ul>
</StorySection>

<StorySection num="08" title="Key *learnings*">
  <ul class="cards">
    <li><p>I used to jump into screens, but now I understand that clear thinking about categories, flows, and user goals should come before design.</p></li>
    <li><p>Something as small as a missing size chart created uncertainty that led to drop-offs. Micro-decisions like these matter deeply to users</p></li>
    <li><p>Features like “View Similar” or “Try On” sound great, but their value only comes through when placed where and when the user actually needs them.</p></li>
    <li><p>Sharing wireframes with peers helped me identify structural issues I would have missed on my own. It saved effort later in UI stages</p></li>
    <li><p>I stopped chasing perfect screens and started focusing on solving small but meaningful user problems with clarity</p></li>
    <li><p>I learned to prioritize what users need most at each stage. Adding multiple categorization types without harmony made the experience chaotic</p></li>
  </ul>
</StorySection>

<StorySection num="09" title="Future *scope*">
  <blockquote>
    <p>When I started this project, I was focused on making clean and useful screens. But as I spent more time with real parents and saw how they actually shopped, something shifted.</p>
    <p>What I’ll take with me into my next project is this: people don’t come to a product looking for features. They come with a question, a doubt, or a deadline. If I can stay focused on that, I know I’ll be designing something that actually helps.</p>
  </blockquote>
</StorySection>
